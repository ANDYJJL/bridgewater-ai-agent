# Filing Analysis Checklist

## Purpose
Use this checklist to analyze filing excerpts for thesis relevance. Filing review is not a transcription exercise. The objective is to identify what changed, what matters, and what those changes imply for the investment case.

## Primary Questions

1. What does the filing say about the business model and demand sources?
2. What does the filing reveal about revenue quality, concentration, and durability?
3. What does the filing suggest about margin structure and cost pressure?
4. What risks are framed as recurring versus newly elevated?
5. What operational constraints could alter the thesis path?

## Sections to Review

### Business overview
Use this section to confirm how management describes the business and where it claims differentiation. Look for changes in emphasis, product mix, or end-market framing.

### Revenue discussion
Identify whether growth came from broadening demand, customer concentration, price, mix, or timing effects. Avoid assuming all growth is equal-quality growth.

### Segment discussion
Check whether the most important business lines are becoming more or less concentrated. High growth concentrated in one segment can strengthen the thesis while still increasing fragility.

### Gross margin and supply chain
Look for evidence on pricing power, ramp costs, manufacturing bottlenecks, or mix shifts. A strong revenue story with weaker margin quality should not be treated as unambiguously supportive.

### Risk factors
Separate boilerplate risk language from areas that appear newly salient. Repeated emphasis on a risk can matter if it lines up with external evidence.

### Customer concentration
Concentration can magnify upside during strong demand periods and magnify downside during digestion periods. Track whether the thesis depends on a narrow set of customers behaving well.

### Inventory and commitments
Inventory, prepayments, and supply commitments often reveal how much future demand the company is implicitly underwriting. This can be supportive or risky depending on demand visibility.

### Management outlook
Use outlook language to identify confidence, caution, bottlenecks, and dependencies. Watch for subtle shifts in wording rather than only explicit guidance changes.

## Decision Rules

1. Extract the facts before writing implications.
2. Distinguish level from direction. A high margin or large segment is less important than whether it is strengthening, weakening, or becoming more volatile.
3. Separate temporary ramp friction from structural deterioration.
4. Treat customer concentration, supply commitments, and policy exposure as interaction risks, not isolated facts.
5. Compare filing evidence with the original thesis assumptions. Do not analyze in a vacuum.

## What Counts as Thesis-Relevant
- a change in the main revenue engine
- a shift in segment concentration
- new evidence on pricing power or margin durability
- stronger dependence on supply chain execution
- higher dependence on a small number of customers
- language suggesting a different adoption timeline

## What Is Usually Noise
- generic boilerplate with no change in emphasis
- isolated wording changes without an operational counterpart
- facts that do not touch demand, economics, risk, or execution

## Handling Conflicting Filing Evidence
If the filing contains both strong demand commentary and cautionary supply or margin language:
- keep both channels visible
- state which one changes near-term results
- state which one changes long-term thesis durability

Do not let strong top-line language erase evidence of concentration or execution fragility.

## Expected Output Structure
The filing-analysis step should produce:
- extracted facts
- candidate metrics for deterministic tools
- thesis implications
- items that require cross-checking with news or macro context

## Escalation Triggers
Escalate when:
- management language changes materially without an obvious quantitative explanation
- customer concentration appears to rise alongside weaker visibility
- supply commitments increase while demand visibility weakens
- there is evidence of structural margin erosion rather than temporary transition costs

The model should act like a disciplined research associate: precise on facts, careful on implications, and explicit about uncertainty.
